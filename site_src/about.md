# About Grid Ops

## What this project is

Grid Ops is a personal exploration by **Ken Rollins**, Federal Field CTO —
Emerging Technologies at Dell, into a single question:

**where does classical simulation of quantum algorithms actually run out, and what does
that mean for the hardware you need to develop them?**

Controlled microgrid islanding was chosen as the anchor problem because it exercises the
interesting parts honestly. It is a genuine combinatorial optimization with a real
physical constraint attached — a partition that looks optimal to the objective function
can still be electrically unsurvivable, and DC power flow is what says so. That gap
between "the optimizer liked it" and "the grid survives it" is most of what makes this
kind of work hard.

The grid problem is the vehicle. The argument is that **developing** a quantum algorithm
is the hard part, that development happens in classical simulation, and that simulation
hits walls you can compute exactly.

## What this project is not

- **Not an official Dell product.** There is nothing to buy. Nothing here is for sale.
- **Not a Dell reference architecture.** This work has not been produced, endorsed, or
  reviewed through any official Dell channel. Views, technical findings, and opinions
  represented here are the author's own and do not represent an official Dell position.
- **Not a claim of quantum advantage.** QAOA has no demonstrated advantage over good
  classical heuristics on problems like this one, and the demonstration shows the cases
  where it **loses** to a classical spectral baseline rather than hiding them.
- **Not a benchmark.** Measured numbers describe specific outcomes on specific hardware in
  specific configurations, and each carries its date and machine. They are honest, but
  they are not normalized comparisons across platforms.
- **Not a quantum computer.** Everything here is *simulation* of quantum algorithms on
  classical GPUs. No QPU, no cloud quantum service, no vendor queue.

## Why this project exists

Above the classical simulation frontier, every failure looks the same. Below it you know
the right answer, so when a result looks wrong you can tell which of three things went
wrong: your algorithm, the hardware's noise, or simply not enough shots. Above it you have
only your answer, and all three are indistinguishable.

That is the case for simulation capacity, and it is not hypothetical here. Two real
algorithm failures were caught in this project **only** because brute force was still
affordable: a deeper circuit scoring worse than a shallow one, and an optimizer reporting
convergence while returning an unevolved state. Neither announced itself. On hardware,
above the frontier, both would have looked like noise — and both would have been believed.

Where a claim in this project turned out to be wrong, the correction is written out rather
than quietly edited, because how a number got fixed is usually more instructive than the
number. Several are recorded in the [technical notes](notes/index.md).

## How the hardware fits in

Dell hardware is referenced throughout because it is what the author works with
day-to-day. The techniques and patterns apply to any platform with comparable
capabilities. The specific machine this ran on is a **Dell Pro Max with GB10 Grace
Blackwell**, 128 GB of unified memory.

That machine was not chosen for peak performance. It was chosen because unified memory
makes the real constraint visible: for state-vector simulation the binding resource is the
largest *coherent* memory domain, not total GPU memory across a box. The
[Hardware](hardware.md) page works that argument through against the rest of the Dell line,
including the cases where a larger machine simulates **fewer** qubits than a smaller one.

## Reproducibility

Figures on this site are generated by the demonstrator's own engine at build time — the
same computation the live application runs, not screenshots of it. The technical notes are
copied from the repository rather than rewritten, so the two cannot drift. Neither the
site build nor CI requires a GPU.
